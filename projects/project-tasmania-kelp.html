Depletion of Kelp Ecosystems on the East Coast of Tasmania

Golden kelp · Long-spined sea urchins · Southern rock lobsters · Treatment methods & trophic cascades

This project examines how two active management strategies—lobster translocation and abalone smashing (urchin culling)—influence populations of Golden kelp (Ecklonia radiata), long-spined sea urchins (Centrostephanus rodgersii), and southern rock lobsters (Jasus edwardsii) along the east coast of Tasmania over a two-year period.

Using secondary belt-transect survey data across treated and control sites, I compressed thousands of observations into a tractable dataset and compared species trajectories under different management regimes. I then used Pearson correlation to quantify how kelp, urchin, and lobster abundances covary across sites and years.

The analysis showed a moderate negative correlation between urchins and kelp, and a moderately strong negative correlation between urchins and lobsters, consistent with a trophic cascade where predator loss enables urchin overgrazing. The lobster–kelp relationship was weaker and positive, suggesting that while lobsters benefit from healthy kelp habitat, their dynamics are also shaped by fishing pressure and climate-driven range shifts.

Overall, the work highlights that short-term interventions can reduce urchin densities locally, but fully restoring kelp biomass likely requires longer timescales, sustained predator recovery, and complementary management that addresses overfishing and warming-driven urchin expansion.

Project details

  • Location: East coast of Tasmania
  • Species: Ecklonia radiata, Centrostephanus rodgersii, Jasus edwardsii
  • Data: Secondary belt-transect surveys across treatment and control sites
  • Methods: data cleaning & compression, treatment comparisons, Pearson correlation
  • Themes: kelp forest resilience, urchin barrens, fishing pressure, climate impacts
